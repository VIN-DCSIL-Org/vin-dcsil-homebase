# Lessons Learned

Building the timer app for teachers changed how our team thought about what it means for a product to be finished. The assignment was not to build a perfect app, but to make an MVP quickly and prove the idea. The CUJ framework helped us focus on the most important user journey: a teacher opening the app, starting a timer, and randomly picking a team during a presentation. That simple framing kept us from getting lost in extra features and pushed us to ask a harder question: what actually creates friction for the user?

Quantifying user friction made the work feel more concrete. Before CUJ, it was easy to treat small annoyances as harmless because they were not technically blockers. Once we started measuring friction, those same issues became visible as costs on the user experience. A button that took too long to find, a confusing layout, or a flow that required too many steps was no longer just “slightly imperfect.” It was something that made the app harder to use in the exact moment it needed to feel effortless. That shifted our perspective from thinking about whether a feature existed to whether the user could complete the task quickly and confidently.

That process also changed how we viewed done versus usable. Done used to mean the core code worked and the main screens were present. Through the CUJ lens, usable meant more than that. It meant the app could be understood instantly, used without explanation, and trusted in a real classroom setting. We realized there is a fine balance between something that is ready to demo and something that is ready to ship. A demo can survive rough edges because the audience is forgiving and the environment is controlled. A shippable product needs consistency, clarity, and polish because the user is not judging it as a project artifact; they are judging it as a tool.

That tension showed up in almost every decision. We had to constantly weigh nice-to-haves against necessary MVP features. Adding visual polish, cleaner spacing, and a more appealing design mattered because aesthetics help sell products and make the experience feel intentional. At the same time, spending too long polishing details could delay the real goal: showing the full functionality as quickly as possible. CUJ helped us make those tradeoffs more honestly. If a change reduced friction in the main flow, it was worth doing. If it only improved appearance without helping the user complete the task, it had to be measured against our time.

One of the biggest lessons was that speed and quality are not always opposites, but they do compete when scope is unclear. CUJ gave us a way to narrow scope without lowering standards. Instead of trying to make everything excellent, we focused on making the critical path excellent enough. That meant the app could be a real MVP: simple, clear, fast, and functional, even if it was not perfect. In the end, the framework taught us that usability is not a bonus added after the fact. It is the part that turns a working project into something people can actually use.
