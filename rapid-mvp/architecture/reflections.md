# Reflections

The move from a local prototype to a production-ready system started with the A2 peer feedback and our CUJ findings. In the prototype stage, it was easy to focus on whether the app technically worked. The feedback made it clear that “works” was not the same as “ready.” Reviewers were not only reacting to missing features; they were reacting to friction in the experience, uncertainty in the workflow, and the gap between a classroom demo and a tool someone could trust during a real presentation. The CUJ process helped us turn that vague criticism into a concrete priority list.

The most important CUJ insight was that our core user is not trying to explore the app. They want to complete one fast action: open the tool, start the timer, and randomly pick a team with minimal hesitation. That sounds simple, but the feedback showed that the journey was carrying avoidable friction. Small delays, unclear affordances, and extra steps mattered because they interrupted the moment the app was supposed to feel effortless. Once we mapped the journey end to end, we could see that the experience depended less on adding new functionality and more on reducing cognitive load, tightening the visual hierarchy, and making the critical path unmistakable.

That reframed our approach to design. Instead of treating polish as optional decoration, we treated it as part of the product’s reliability. A cleaner interface, clearer spacing, and more deliberate presentation now matter because they help users understand the state of the app instantly. The CUJ findings also pushed us to think beyond the visible screen. If the app is going to be used in front of a class, it needs to behave predictably, recover gracefully, and avoid looking fragile. That is what moved our thinking toward a production mindset: not just whether the code runs locally, but whether the system feels stable under real use.

The peer feedback also highlighted architectural concerns that were easy to ignore in a sandbox. A local build can rely on manual setup, developer-specific assumptions, and hidden state. A production-grade system cannot. That is why the cloud transition forced us to separate environment-specific configuration from application logic, plan for secret management, and make deployment repeatable rather than manual. Those changes were not just operational upgrades; they were direct responses to the need for consistency and trust. If a user is expected to rely on the app during an important moment, then the deployment path has to be as dependable as the UI.

Another major lesson was learning to separate MVP needs from future scalability. The feedback did not ask for over-engineering. It asked for a clearer, safer, and more complete experience. That meant we needed to solve the immediate problems well before imagining a larger platform. We focused on the essentials: a stable release, a clearer flow, secure handling of sensitive information, and an architecture that could grow without being redesigned from scratch. In other words, we tried to build a foundation instead of a shortcut.

Overall, the A2 feedback and CUJ analysis changed our definition of progress. A better product was not one with more code or more screens. It was one that removed friction from the exact moments that matter, behaved predictably in a real environment, and gave users confidence instead of doubt. That is what turned the project from a functional local prototype into something much closer to a production system.
